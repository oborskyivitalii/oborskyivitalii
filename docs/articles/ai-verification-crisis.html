AI, the Externalization of Reasoning, and the Verification Crisis

Source edition: Agile Insider on Medium, 12 June 2026. Also published on LinkedIn on 10 June 2026.

Vitalii Oborskyi · Article

About 3 min read

Contents
  1. The Historical Analogy: Externalizing Memory
  2. The Verification Thought Experiment
  3. The Core Bottleneck of the AI Era

AI is not just another step in software automation. It represents a much deeper technological shift: one of the first large-scale attempts to delegate parts of reasoning-like work, judgment, synthesis, and code production to machines.

To understand where we are heading, we need to look at the macro-historical context of engineering and technology. Computers delegated calculation. The internet changed access, distribution, and coordination. Software automated workflows and virtualized work that previously lived only in the physical world. But AI moves closer to a far more sensitive layer: the space where humans interpret, decide, explain, write, and reason.

The Historical Analogy: Externalizing Memory

The closest historical analogy to artificial intelligence may not be the computer or the internet. It may be the invention of writing itself.

When humans developed writing, reading, and later printing, we externalized memory. For the first time, ideas could outlive their authors, travel across physical borders, and be reproduced at scale. That single capability fundamentally reshaped civilization. Yet, shifts this deep are notoriously hard to measure in real-time. How many conflicts, movements, institutions, or ideological transformations were shaped simply by our ability to preserve and distribute ideas through text? It is almost impossible to know. Technologies of this caliber do not just optimize tasks; they reshape the baseline systems through which society thinks, remembers, organizes, and acts.

However, writing took centuries and millennia for humanity to absorb. We had time to develop literacy, build schools, establish editors and reviewers, design legal systems, and cultivate cultural norms around written knowledge. With AI, we do not have millennia. We barely have years.

The Verification Thought Experiment

To understand the hidden risk of this speed, imagine a simple thought experiment. Suppose humans gradually delegated the entire acts of reading and writing to an AI system. People would speak, and the system would write. Later, people would ask a question, and the system would read the answer back.

At first glance, this looks like ultimate efficiency. But if humans lose the ability to read and write well enough to verify the system, the tool transforms into an absolute black box. Did it record what we actually said? Did it preserve the original meaning? Is it reading back the stored text, or simply generating a plausible version on the fly? Who can check the output if the fundamental verification skill has disappeared from the population?

Now, apply this exact pattern to modern software engineering.

The Core Bottleneck of the AI Era

If AI assistants generate more and more code, but human engineers lose the ability, time, or organizational capacity to understand, review, challenge, and maintain it, we create the exact same failure mode. The problem is not that AI writes code. The problem emerges when code generation scales faster than code comprehension, review capacity, architectural ownership, and long-term maintenance literacy.

AI can be an extraordinary amplifier for engineering teams, but only if we intentionally preserve the human and organizational capacity to verify what it produces. The real risk to the industry is not automation itself. The real risk is delegating a critical capability faster than we preserve the ability to audit, correct, and govern it.

This is one of the core ideas behind my Uncertainty Architecture work. Reliability is not merely a model problem; it is a system, team, and operating model problem.

The model can propose. The system must verify. The team must still own the loop.
